Each October, the mountains surrounding Michoacán came alive. Nestled in a valley, a little village in Mexico lay cradled between the hills. Halloween, or the Night of the Witches, was tolerated there. Teenagers strung up black balloons and shouted “¡Feliz Halloween!” as they ran through the cobblestone streets in skull masks.

Marisol sat on a low stool in the courtyard, threading marigolds onto thin wire. The petals stained her fingertips yellow-orange, like sunlight blushing against her skin. Her daughters ran in and out of the house, carrying baskets of petals and laughing about the costumes they had seen in town. One wanted to be a witch, another a vampire. Marisol smiled without looking up. She let them chatter, now accustomed to the rhythm of this new world rubbing against the old.

Marisol was still arranging the marigolds when her mother came in through the side gate.

“Mira, look at them,” Ines said, glancing toward the side street.

A pack of boys ran past, one of them holding a plastic scythe that bent in the wind. They laughed.

“They dress like the dead, but they forget what it means,” Ines murmured.

She lowered herself onto the bench near Marisol, her knees stiff, her shawl slipping from one shoulder.

“Death used to be sacred. We fed it. We talked with it. Now it’s a joke, a costume from America.”

Marisol kept her eyes on her work. The wire cut a faint groove into her thumb.

“It’s only play, Mama,” she said.

Ines snorted.

“Play at forgetting. You don’t seem to remember last year. What happened to Eduardo? It wasn’t just play anymore. When your son—”

“Mama, stop it,” Marisol said.

Her mother fell silent.

Ines rose slowly, brushing the dust from her skirt.

“He will be back with the others tomorrow, on el Día de los Niños,” she said.

Marisol did not move. The courtyard seemed to grow still with her. The basin of petals was half in shadow now. The last light caught their edges, making them look like small embers. These would show Eduardo the way.

A moth struck one of the candles on the wall, then fell soundlessly into the wax.

Marisol lifted a marigold to her face and pressed it against her nose. Her eyes filled with tears.

“Ah, Dios mío, please bring him back home with you. Dear wind, carry his tired wings. Dear water, give him strength.”

She pressed the flower to her eyes and sobbed.

The wind stirred, lifting a few loose petals into the courtyard. They spun briefly, and Marisol watched them until her vision blurred again and everything orange turned to flame. Then she smelled it once more: the smoke that had never truly left her.

It had been exactly one year since it happened.

The air had been full of cheap laughter and foreign ghosts. The teenagers had gone to the old sugar mill outside the village, carrying candles and bottles, playing la carrera de las almas, the race of souls. They dared one another to run through the mill’s hollow corridors with a lit candle, whispering the names of the dead. If the flame stayed alive, your soul was pure. If it went out, something had claimed you.

Eduardo was sixteen. He had run last, promising he would keep his candle burning longer than anyone else’s.

He had.

The fire caught quickly: the wind, the dry cane walls, the spilled liquor. By the time the others reached him, the roof had already given way. He lay burning beneath a timber plank, his candle still alight beside him.

Marisol saw it all later, in the dark folds of her dreams: the orange blossoming, the black collapsing. She pressed her palms over her eyes until only darkness remained.

“Tomorrow, hijo, you’ll return with all the other monarchs,” she whispered.

She imagined him tracing the jewels of the mountains with his painted wings, adorning the trees before reaching their valley at last.

The night Eduardo died, his soul was carried north, and for an entire year it travelled, following the ancient path of the monarchs.

Tomorrow, it would find its way home.
